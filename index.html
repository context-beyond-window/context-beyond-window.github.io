Context Beyond the Window

Compression, Accumulation, and Internalization of Knowledge in Language Models

COLM 2026 Workshop · October 9, 2026 · San Francisco, CA

About

Modern language models operate within finite context windows, yet many real-world tasks require models to absorb, retain, and act on information that far exceeds any single prompt. This workshop focuses on the full spectrum of context management: methods that fit more useful information into the context window, methods that maintain or accumulate state across long interactions, and methods that transfer contextual knowledge into model parameters.

Our goal is to create a common venue for researchers asking a shared question: how should language models acquire, compress, store, and access knowledge, and when should each mechanism be used? A useful lens is the trade-off between context-time memory (information supplied at inference) and weight-time memory (information absorbed into parameters).

Context compression & summarization Long-context architectures Memory-augmented & recurrent models Retrieval-augmented generation Multi-turn context management Agentic memory & orchestration Knowledge distillation & internalization Continual & lifelong learning KV cache optimization State space models Benchmarks & evaluation Test-time training & memorization

Yoshua Bengio is a Turing Award laureate, Professor at Université de Montréal, Founder of Mila, and Scientific Director of LawZero. His recent work focuses on AI safety, including managing the risks of advanced AI systems, and on how language models can safely acquire, store, and act on knowledge.

Avi Singh is a Research Scientist at Google DeepMind (PhD, UC Berkeley). He co-authored the Many-Shot In-Context Learning paper demonstrating how expanded context windows enable significant performance gains without fine-tuning, bridging RL and long-context LLM capabilities.

Aakanksha Chowdhery is Co-Founder of Reflection AI and Adjunct Professor at Stanford. She was technical lead on PaLM (540B) and lead researcher for Gemini pre-training at Google, bringing deep expertise in large-scale pre-training and knowledge internalization.

Omar Khattab is an Assistant Professor at MIT EECS & CSAIL and creator of ColBERT and DSPy. His research directly addresses retrieval-augmented pipelines—how external knowledge is retrieved, compressed, and fed into language model context.

Yejin Choi is the Dieter Schwarz Foundation Professor at Stanford CS, Senior Director at NVIDIA Research, and a TIME100 AI honoree. Her research spans commonsense reasoning and knowledge representation, with a focus on scaling intelligence via smarter algorithms rather than brute-force context expansion.

Albert Gu is an Assistant Professor at CMU and Chief Scientist at Cartesia AI. He created the S4 and Mamba architectures for linear-time sequence modeling, which are foundational to how context can be compressed and managed beyond the transformer paradigm.

  • Submission portal: OpenReview (link TBA)
Submission Types

  • Position papers
  • System descriptions

Each submission will receive at least two reviews. The review process is double-blind. Organizers will not review papers for which they have a conflict of interest. Accepted work will be presented as contributed talks or posters, with oral slots selected to ensure strong representation of junior researchers.

Panel Discussion

The Memory Bottleneck: When Should Knowledge Live in Context vs. Weights?

Moderator: Dwarkesh Patel — host of the Dwarkesh Podcast (TIME100 AI 2024), author of The Scaling Era (Stripe Press, 2025)

In his widely discussed essay "Thoughts on AI Progress" (December 2025), Patel argued that continual learning—the ability to move knowledge from context into weights—is the critical unsolved bottleneck for AI progress, sparking a multi-month debate among ML researchers. He brings deep technical preparation and a talent for surfacing disagreements between experts, having conducted in-depth interviews with researchers at the frontier of scaling (Sutskever, Amodei), interpretability (Douglas, Bricken), and architecture design (Karpathy).

The panel will bring together all six invited speakers to debate: when should knowledge be compressed in context, retrieved from external stores, or internalized into weights? What are the safety implications of each? And is a unified framework possible?
